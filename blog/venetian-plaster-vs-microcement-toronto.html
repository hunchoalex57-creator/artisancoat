Venetian Plaster vs Microcement Walls in Toronto: Which Textured Finish Actually Belongs in Your Home

Houzz's 2026 trend data has one clear headline: people are done with flat drywall. Venetian plaster searches are up 94 percent, limewash 53 percent, stone 148 percent. Across the GTA, homeowners are asking for the same two finishes by name — and most of them don't know which one they actually want.

Something shifted in Toronto interiors this year. Walk through a new build in Vaughan or a gut reno in Leaside and the walls aren't painted — they're finished. Troweled. Mottled. They catch light and move with it.

The data backs up what we're seeing on site. Houzz's 2026 trend report puts Venetian plaster searches up 94 percent year over year, with limewash up 53 percent and stone up 148 percent. Texture, not colour, is driving the 2026 design conversation. And in the GTA, that conversation almost always narrows to two products: Venetian plaster and microcement.

They look similar in a photo. They are not the same material, they don't cost the same, and putting the wrong one in the wrong room is an expensive mistake. Here's the honest breakdown.

What Venetian Plaster Actually Is

Venetian plaster is lime-based. Slaked lime and finely ground marble dust, burnished in multiple thin coats with a steel trowel until the surface develops depth — that wet, luminous, almost-marble look that changes as you move past it.

It's old technology done well. Because it's lime, it's breathable, naturally mould-resistant and highly alkaline. Because it's burnished rather than sealed, the finish has an optical quality no paint reproduces. A single wall can read charcoal from one angle and warm grey from another.

What it isn't: waterproof. Traditional Venetian plaster is a wall material. It's not built for floors, it's not built for a shower enclosure, and it doesn't take abrasion.

What Microcement Actually Is

Microcement is a modern polymer-modified cementitious coating — typically 2 to 3 mm total, applied over a primer and reinforcing mesh, then sealed with a polyurethane topcoat.

That sealer is the whole difference. Once cured, a microcement surface is genuinely waterproof, chemical-resistant and hard enough to walk on. Which is why in Toronto homes it goes everywhere lime plaster can't: shower walls, wet-room floors, kitchen splashbacks, basement slabs, stair treads, countertops.

It bonds over almost anything stable — existing tile, concrete, drywall, plywood, old plaster — and it adds so little thickness that you keep your door clearances and your ceiling height. No demolition, no dumpster, no grout.

"We asked for Venetian plaster in the ensuite because we'd seen it on Instagram. Artisan Coat talked us out of it and put microcement in instead — same look, but it's a wet room with two kids in it. Two years on, there isn't a mark on it. Then they did Venetian in the dining room, where it belonged." — Homeowner, Oakville

The Real Difference Isn't the Look — It's Where It Goes

Ninety percent of the choice comes down to one question: does this surface get wet, walked on, or scrubbed?

  • Dry feature walls, dining rooms, bedrooms, hallways, ceilings. Venetian plaster wins on pure beauty. Nothing matches burnished lime for depth and light play.
  • Bathrooms, showers, kitchens, laundry, basements, floors, stairs. Microcement, without debate. It's the only one of the two rated for water and traffic.
  • Fireplace surrounds. Either — both handle radiant heat well. Choose on look and budget.
  • Whole-home continuity. Microcement, because it's the only one that can run from the wall onto the floor as one unbroken surface.
  • Heritage or solid-masonry homes in older Toronto neighbourhoods. Lime plaster, because breathability matters on those walls.

Artisan Coat Note

Be careful with quotes under $12 per square foot for anything called "Venetian plaster." At that price it's almost always a textured acrylic paint product rolled on and glazed — it photographs fine on day one and looks plastic within two years. Real burnished lime plaster is three to five coats of skilled trowel work. The labour alone makes the low number impossible.

Going Over Tile, Concrete and Old Walls

This is the question we get most often from Mississauga and Brampton homeowners mid-renovation: do we have to rip out the tile first?

For microcement, no. A sound, well-bonded tile wall is a perfectly good substrate. The joints get filled, the surface is primed, mesh goes in over the grout lines to control differential movement, and the microcement builds from there. Two to three days for a bathroom, versus a week of demolition, dust and disposal fees.

For Venetian plaster, the substrate needs to be flat, dry and stable — drywall, existing plaster, or masonry. It can be applied over tile with the right base coat, but it's rarely the right call, because if the room has tile in it, it's usually a room that gets wet.

What neither finish will fix: an active moisture problem. If a Scarborough basement wall is wicking water or a Toronto shower has been leaking behind the tile for two years, that gets solved first. Coating over moisture just relocates the failure.

Living With Them in a GTA Home

Maintenance. Sealed microcement wipes down with mild soap and water; the topcoat is refreshed every 8 to 12 years in heavy-use areas. Venetian plaster is dusted or wiped gently and can be waxed to renew depth. Neither needs repainting.

Repairs. Both are patchable, but both are hand-applied, so a patch is a blend, not a swap. This is the argument for hiring someone who'll still be in business in five years.

Resale. In the Toronto market, a seamless microcement bathroom or a plastered feature wall reads as a custom finish, not a cosmetic upgrade. Buyers price it that way.

Winter. Both are interior products here. Ontario freeze-thaw is why we don't recommend either on exposed exterior walls — that's what our frost-rated exterior systems are for.

What to Ask Before You Hire in the GTA

  • "What exact product system are you using?" A real answer names a manufacturer and a system. A vague answer means acrylic paint.
  • "Show me a sample wall you finished at least three years ago." Anyone can make a fresh sample board look good.
  • "How many coats, and what's the sealer?" For microcement in a wet area, you want a two-component polyurethane topcoat, minimum two coats.
  • "Is mesh going in?" Over tile or any jointed substrate, mesh is not optional.
  • "What's the warranty, in writing?" Ours is three years. Ask for the paper.
  • "Can I see a wet, sealed sample in my own light?" These finishes shift dramatically between dry and sealed, and between showroom light and your north-facing living room.

The Short Answer

If it's a dry wall you want people to stop and look at, Venetian plaster. If it's a surface that has to survive water, feet, kids or cleaning products — or if you want one continuous finish running from wall to floor — microcement.

Most of the GTA projects we finish end up using both. Lime plaster where the light lands, microcement where the water goes. That's not a compromise; that's using each material for what it's actually good at.
